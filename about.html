About the studio

A small team, run like a software house.

We came together to solve one specific problem — a logistics and warehousing company whose staff needed safer, clearer training than a handbook could give them. Five of us, five roles, one delivery standard.

Our story

Why we exist

We were brought in by a logistics and warehousing company in the automotive sector to design and build an Interactive Health & Safety Training Platform — a 360° experience that helps warehouse staff learn hazard zones, PPE requirements and emergency procedures before they ever set foot on site, with a VR-based extension planned as a future phase.

Alongside the platform, we built this website and the identity behind it, because how a studio presents itself says as much about its standards as the product it ships.

How we work

Our operating rules

We run an agile, client-focused process: regular internal meetings, structured documentation, and a clear division of responsibility, so our work is accountable to both our client and the standards of a professional delivery team.

  • Every progress meeting is minuted, without exception
  • Every team member owns a named area — no shared ambiguity
  • Client feedback is logged and actioned within the following sprint
  • Regular personal reflections keep the whole team honest about progress

Corporate identity

The mark, explained

Every artefact we ship — this site, our presentations, our documentation — carries the same shield mark and system, so NexaTech reads as one consistent brand rather than five people's separate work.

See the full brand identity page →

The shield

The hexagonal shield shape represents protection and safety — the exact promise our client's warehouse staff need from their training. It's the same silhouette used for every icon and photo frame across the site.

Palette & type

A technical, industrial system

Blue reads as trustworthy and technical; amber is reserved for the safety-critical accents, echoing hazard signage without leaning on cliché. Space Grotesk gives headings an angular, structural edge; IBM Plex Mono labels data the way a spec sheet would.
